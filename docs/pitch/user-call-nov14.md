**The honest part, which a judge may ask about** (23:26): "I don't know how much traction it would actually get
with deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals, if I'm going to be honest. I feel like it would be more popular with
... children in general [with] speech impediments." Her reason: in speech therapy for deaf children a lot is done
by touch (hands on the throat and mouth). Our answer: that is why we show mouth pictures, the video and the
meter, why vibration is in the plan, and why we pitch it as practice between sessions with a therapist, not in
place of one.

## What she told us

- The app may suit **children** (about kindergarten to 3rd grade) more than deaf adults, with a **parent and child
  doing it together**.
- **Partner with speech therapists:** let them assign homework and lessons to their clients.
- Visual learning matters most: **mouth pictures** and video, because deaf users rely on visual and touch cues
  (vibration), not sound.
- It may be popular with **speech impediments in general**, not only deaf and hard-of-hearing learners.
- Add **"r" and "th"** sounds.
- Train on **many voices and accents**, including the Deaf accent.
- **Gamify it:** rewards such as confetti when a sound is right.
- Show **mouth diagrams** (the side view with the tongue's place), which people in speech therapy already know (29:58).
- Resources she named: ADWAS and Lifeprint.
- She offered to connect us with people in the Deaf and hard-of-hearing community and the medical field for
  testing and feedback.
